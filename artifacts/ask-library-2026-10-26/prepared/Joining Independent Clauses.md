When connecting two independent clauses (groups of words that can stand alone as complete sentences), there are three primary methods to manage the transition. Your choice depends on the desired rhythm and the strength of the connection between the ideas.

## 1. Semicolons (;)
A semicolon is used when two thoughts are closely related in meaning and you want to emphasize that connection without using a coordinating conjunction. It provides a stronger break than a comma but a softer break than a period.
*   *The rain stopped; the sun began to peek through the clouds.*
*   *Some people prefer tea; others prefer coffee.*

## 2. Comma and a Conjunction (, + FANBOYS)
This is the standard method for joining related thoughts while clearly defining their relationship (addition, contrast, or cause/effect). Use a comma followed by one of the coordinating conjunctions: **for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so**.
*   **And (Addition):** She finished her presentation, and the audience erupted in applause.
*   **But (Contrast):** He studied very hard, but he still felt nervous about the exam.
*   **So (Result):** The store was closed, so we went home instead.

## 3. No Punctuation (Run-on Sentences)
Generally, you should avoid joining two independent clauses with no punctuation at all. This creates a "run-on sentence," which is confusing because it fails to signal where one thought ends and the next begins.
*   *Incorrect: The gate swung open the crowd rushed in.*

### The Exception: Short, Parallel Clauses
You may use a comma alone (technically a comma splice) only when the clauses are very short and balanced. This creates a rhythmic, poetic effect similar to a list.
*   *He smiled, he waved, he walked away.*
*   *No more lies, no more fear, no more pain.*

## Rule of Thumb
If you are unsure whether a comma is sufficient to join two full sentences, **use a period or a semicolon.** It is always safer to be grammatically clear than to risk a run-on sentence.
