# Gerund vs Infinitive Verb Patterns

**Date**: 2026-05-28 16:49

## Core Distinction
Gerunds (`-ing` forms acting as nouns) generally feel concrete, real, ongoing, or completed. Infinitives (`to + verb`) feel abstract, potential, or forward-looking. This underlying difference in time and reality drives most usage patterns.

## Structural Rules
Certain grammatical positions strictly dictate the form:
- **Gerunds** are used when the verb acts as the **subject** (`Reading helps you relax.`) or follows a **preposition** (`I'm good at drawing.` / `Thanks for coming.`).
- **Infinitives** are used to show **purpose** (`I went to the store to buy milk.`) or follow an **adjective** (`It's easy to forget.` / `I'm happy to help.`).

## Verb-Specific Patterns: Learn by Meaning
English doesn't have a single rule for which main verb takes which form, but verbs cluster predictably by semantic theme. Instead of memorizing alphabetical lists, group them by concept:

**Gerund-Only Verbs** (focus on actual experience, avoidance, completion, or consideration)
- *Enjoyment/Dislike:* `enjoy`, `appreciate`, `mind`, `detest` → `I enjoy hiking.`
- *Avoidance/Delay:* `avoid`, `postpone`, `risk`, `put off` → `She avoided answering.`
- *Completion/Continuation:* `finish`, `quit`, `keep (on)` → `He finished writing.`
- *Consideration/Suggestion:* `consider`, `imagine`, `suggest`, `admit` → `They suggested leaving.`

**Infinitive-Only Verbs** (focus on desire, intention, decisions, or future effort)
- *Desire/Hope:* `want`, `hope`, `wish`, `long` → `We hope to visit.`
- *Planning/Decisions:* `plan`, `decide`, `choose`, `promise`, `refuse` → `She decided to apply.`
- *Effort/Ability/Tendency:* `attempt`, `manage`, `afford`, `fail`, `seem` → `I can't afford to wait.`

## Verbs That Change Meaning
A small group of verbs accepts both forms, but the choice shifts the meaning based on the core time/reality distinction:
- **`stop`**: `He stopped smoking.` (Quit the habit.) vs. `He stopped to smoke.` (Paused another action in order to smoke.)
- **`remember` / `forget`**: `I remember locking the door.` (Memory of a past action.) vs. `Remember to lock the door.` (Forward-looking reminder.)
- **`try`**: `Try restarting the router.` (Experiment with a method.) vs. `Try to restart the router.` (Make an effort; success is uncertain.)
- **`regret`**: `I regret telling him.` (Remorse over a past action.) vs. `I regret to inform you…` (Formal preface to bad news.)
- **`mean`**: `This means working weekends.` (Involves/results in.) vs. `I meant to call you.` (Intended/planned.)
- **`go on`**: `He went on talking.` (Continued the same action.) vs. `He went on to become a doctor.` (Moved to a new stage.)

## Rule of Thumb & Learning Strategy
When choosing between forms, ask: **Am I looking back at something real, or forward toward a goal?**
- Real/experienced/finished activity → **gerund**
- Future/intended/potential action or purpose → **infinitive**

Anchor new verbs to their meaning category rather than memorizing them in isolation. When in doubt, check a learner dictionary for pattern tags like `[+ -ing]` or `[+ to infinitive]`. The semantic logic holds up across most of English, making the right choice intuitive over time.
